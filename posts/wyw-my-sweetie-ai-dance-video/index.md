## How the dance reads on camera

A dance clip succeeds when the viewer can read the performer’s body language at phone size. Full-body framing gives the knees, feet, shoulders, and hand accents room to register. If your source is a tight portrait, choose a head-and-shoulders groove instead of asking a video model to invent visible footwork beyond the crop.

Think in beats rather than a long list of moves. First, establish a relaxed stance. Next, add one grounded knee bounce or low dip. Then choose a small upper-body accent, such as a shoulder shrug or arm hit. Finish with a held look, a smile, or a partner entrance. These are broad motion ingredients for an original take, not a promise to reproduce a choreographer’s exact routine.

A three-beat plan is also easier to evaluate after generation. If the first movement happens before the viewer understands the pose, open with a fraction of stillness. If the knees disappear at the bottom of the frame, widen the source or reduce the movement. If the ending feels weak, hold the final expression for a moment rather than adding a fourth move.

## Prepare a source image that leaves room to move

Choose a clear, well-lit image with the intended subject large enough to recognize and enough space around the body for the requested action. A full-body or three-quarter photo works better for a knee bounce than a cropped face. Keep hands, shoes, and the top of the head inside the composition if they matter to the joke. A simple background can help, but good separation between subject and background matters more than an empty wall.

Use an image you own, an original illustration, or a photo you have permission to upload and transform. If another adult is recognizable, get consent for the likeness and the public use. Avoid private photos, unlicensed screenshots, and images that make a real person appear to perform a dance they did not do. If your source subject is a child, do not turn a trend prompt into a public likeness experiment; choose an original fictional character instead.

Before uploading, look at the image in the intended vertical crop. Leave headroom above the hair and space beside the shoulders. Keep the face toward the camera or a gentle three-quarter angle, and avoid motion blur, heavy filters, sunglasses, or clothing patterns that compete with the body. The image model may reinterpret a source, so a clean input makes drift easier to notice.

## Write a motion prompt that stays readable

A useful prompt gives the model an ordered scene, not a pile of aesthetic adjectives. State who is visible, what one movement happens, how the camera behaves, and what must remain stable. The phrase “energetic dance” is too open-ended by itself; pairing it with one grounded movement and a steady frame gives you a better review target.

Start with the subject and framing. Then describe the action in simple verbs: “bend,” “bounce,” “shift,” “settle.” Add the mood and background only after the action is clear. Avoid asking for exact choreography from a named creator or an exact music sync that the selected model may not support. Add audio in editing if the workflow generates silent footage.

Use the following prompt as a broad movement direction, not a copy of a specific creator’s sequence:

```text
Animate the adult subject in the reference image with an original, playful
Jersey Club-inspired groove. Keep the same person, outfit, and bright studio.
Start with a relaxed standing pose, add two small knee bounces and a light
shoulder shrug, then shift one step to the side and smile at the camera.
Keep the full body in frame, use natural anatomy, and hold a steady vertical
camera. Do not add another person, change clothes, add text, or create audio.
```

A second option can create a slower, more expressive take. Keep the rhythm broad so the output remains an interpretation rather than a demand for exact replication:

```text
Create a short original dance-style motion from this full-body photo. The
fictional performance has a soft bounce, one low knee dip, relaxed shoulders,
and a small hand accent near the chest. Maintain the subject's visible face,
clothing, and room. Use one continuous shot with a gentle camera push-in only.
No cuts, no extra limbs, no new text, and no lip-sync.
```

For a duet idea, write the interaction without assigning a real person a humiliating or unwanted role. If the workflow accepts just one image, include both consenting adults in that image and check the selected model’s actual inputs first:

```text
In this original, friendly two-person dance scene, the first adult leads one
simple knee bounce and shoulder accent. The second adult joins beside them on
the next beat, and both settle into a relaxed side-by-side pose. Keep both
faces and full bodies visible, maintain their clothing and relative positions,
and keep the camera fixed. No grabbing, no identity swap, no words, and no
sound generation.
```

When the input is an illustration rather than a photograph, make the artistic direction explicit and keep the action small:

```text
Animate this original illustrated character in a light, confident groove:
one gentle bounce, a subtle shoulder roll, and a short side step. Preserve the
character design, colors, face, and background style. Keep the camera steady
and the whole character visible. Use smooth, physically plausible movement;
do not add new characters, logos, or lettering.
```

If the model keeps inventing movement, reduce the request to one action and one camera instruction:

```text
Keep the same adult subject and scene. Make one gentle knee bounce, then hold
a relaxed smile. Full-body vertical framing, stable camera, natural movement.
Do not add a spin, jump, second dancer, text, outfit change, or camera cut.
```

## Solo, duo, or character version?

A solo clip is the simplest way to test the movement. It gives the model one face and one body to keep stable. A duo adds a relationship and a timing decision: should both dancers move together, should one enter later, or should the second person react? Choose one of those ideas rather than asking for all three.

A character version can work if the audience understands that the subject is fictional. Use original artwork or an avatar you control. A dance prompt that animates a real person without permission creates a different problem from a fictional meme, even if the clip is meant as a joke. For a group, confirm that everyone is comfortable with the edit and the gesture before publishing.

## Troubleshoot common WYW My Sweetie video problems

**The feet disappear.** Start with a full-body image and leave more room below the shoes. If the model still crops the movement, ask for a smaller upper-body groove instead of distant footwork.

**The body changes between frames.** Reduce the number of actions, avoid a fast spin, and use a clean source with visible clothing and lighting. A model may still alter a face or outfit; regenerate or choose a different source rather than presenting a distorted version as faithful footage.

**The dance looks too stiff.** Ask for a single relaxed bounce and a small shoulder accent, not “perfect viral choreography.” Try a source with a natural standing posture and avoid a pose that hides the arms. Review the next draft from start to finish.

**The output does not follow the song.** That can happen when the model produces silent video or does not receive the audio as a supported control. Add the permitted sound after generation and make the cut in an editor. Do not claim exact synchronization unless you checked it.

**The duo feels crowded.** Use a wider source with both adults already visible, keep each person on one side of the frame, and choose a side-by-side groove. If the workflow only accepts one reference image, do not pretend it accepted two separate photos.

**The clip looks like a real dance recording.** Say that it is AI-assisted or staged when the context could mislead viewers. Avoid a caption that credits the original choreographer for movement the model invented, or credits a generator for movement performed by a real dancer.
